October was the month I stopped working out, stopped reading, stopped doing anything that needed willpower past 7pm. Job was fine. Life was fine. I was just out of fuel.

The two-minute rule is the only habit that made it through.

The idea is dumb in how simple it is. If you want to start exercising, the habit isn't "go to the gym." It's "put on your gym clothes." That's it. Two minutes. Stop there if you want. No guilt. You still did the habit.

I'd read it in James Clear's book a year earlier and rolled my eyes at the time. I tried it in October because I had nothing left.

Here's what I noticed in November.

Putting on shoes doesn't magically make you want to run. What it does is shrink the decision to something so small your brain can't generate an excuse against it. After four or five days of doing the tiny version, the bigger version starts feeling possible again. Not because you're inspired. Because you have evidence that the first step is survivable.

The versions I actually used:

- **Reading.** Open the book to the bookmark. Read one sentence.
- **Writing.** Open the doc, type one line, save.
- **Walking.** Stand near the front door with shoes on.
- **Side projects.** `git pull`, type one line, quit.

Looks pathetic. That's the whole feature.

The thing about burnout that nobody tells you: you can't push your way out. You're already running on a battery that prints negative numbers. Pushing makes it worse. What you can do is lower the bar so far that crossing it costs almost nothing. After enough days of crossing it, the bar can rise on its own again.

By the end of November I was running three days a week. Reading at night. Pushing commits on the side. None of it felt like willpower. It felt like the habit was carrying me instead of the other way around.

I'm not a habit guru. The next stressful season will probably knock me out again. But I trust the two-minute rule now in a way I didn't before. It's the seatbelt I leave clicked even on good days.

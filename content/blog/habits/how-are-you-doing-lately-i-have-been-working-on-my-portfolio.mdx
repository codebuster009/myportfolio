Rebuilt my portfolio for the fourth time this month. Most weeks I don't have fourth-time-portfolio energy in me. This one was different.

The old version looked like a recruiter dating profile. Hero with my name in 70px font. Three big buttons. A grid of projects with one-line descriptions and a "Hire Me" tab in the header. Clean, sleek, forgettable. Identical to every other React dev's site I've ever clicked on.

I closed the tab on my own portfolio one day and couldn't remember a single thing about it five minutes later. That was the signal.

The new version is a writing-first site. Hero says I build things and also keep notes. There's a Now page. A blog with categories like *habits* and *travel* alongside *systems* and *learning*. A Vibe section with whatever album I'm playing this week. A bookshelf. A polaroid strip of moments I don't want to forget.

The work is still there. Projects, case studies, services page for people who want to hire me. But the proportions are different now. The work is one section of a person, not the whole person.

A friend asked me why I bothered. The pragmatic answer, for someone trying to build a freelance practice: a personal site that feels personal converts way better than a generic dev site. Founders hire people they trust. People they feel like they know. The bento boxes and the field notes and the slightly self-deprecating jokes aren't decoration. They're the trust.

The truer answer is: I'm tired of pretending to be a brand. I'm a person who happens to write code for a living. I read books, eat the same coffee at the same place too many days in a row, have opinions about TypeScript that are mildly unhinged. I'd rather a site reflect that than another silicon-valley-pitch-deck-as-a-portfolio.

If you're rebuilding yours right now, the only thing I'd suggest: put the boring sales pages second, not first. Lead with whatever feels true. The hireability follows.

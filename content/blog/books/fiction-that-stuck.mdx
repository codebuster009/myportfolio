I don't usually read science fiction. My fiction shelf is mostly literary stuff and translated novels, which means I spend a lot of time pretending I understood the metaphor. So when a friend pushed Andy Weir's *Project Hail Mary* into my hands last February and said "just read it," I was skeptical.

I read it in a single weekend. Haven't stopped talking about it since.

No spoilers. Here's the thing about that book.

The protagonist wakes up on a spaceship with no memory of who he is or how he got there. The first two hundred pages are him figuring it out, mostly using high-school physics and a stubborn refusal to panic. There's something deeply satisfying about reading a character whose superpower is patient problem-solving. Not magic. Not chosen-one mythology. Just thinking carefully, taking notes, being willing to be wrong.

It's a book that respects its reader. The science is real enough that the puzzles feel earned. The pacing is brisk without being cheap. The humor is dry and self-aware, the kind I tend to like in code reviews. And it has, somewhere in the middle, one of the most moving depictions of friendship I've read in any genre.

What surprised me was how much it changed the way I read after. I'd been on a steady diet of books where the prose was the point. Sentence-level pleasure. *Project Hail Mary* reminded me that storytelling itself, the actual moving of someone from point A to point B, is its own craft and an undervalued one.

Since then I've read *The Martian* (same author, also great) and most of *Children of Time* (different author, similar texture). The sci-fi shelf is no longer the embarrassed corner of my library. Turns out a good story is a good story.

If you haven't read it, just read it. Skip the movie until you have. Book's better.

I'm from Himachal. Manali specifically. People who didn't grow up in the mountains tend to assume it must be permanent vacation. People who did grow up in the mountains know it's mostly being cold, mostly being out of network, and mostly waiting for the bus.

I go back roughly every six months. April is the best time because the snow at higher altitude is still there but the valley itself is warming up. Apple orchards are flowering. The rivers are loud. Everyone's relieved to be wearing fewer layers.

The drive from Delhi or Chandigarh is the part most people complain about and the part I love. Once you cross Mandi the road starts climbing and the air changes. You can feel it in your ears before you can see it through the window. Cars rented in Delhi start groaning on the inclines. I've been in maybe a hundred of those cars over the years. The groan is the same every time.

What's strange about going home is how aware you become of how much it's changed. The cafe near the bus stand that wasn't there last year. The new layer of tourist hotels along the road to Solang. The Mall Road getting somehow louder every visit. I used to roll my eyes at people who complained about Manali "selling out", but I get it now. I get it and I still come back. Maybe that's how home works.

The non-tourist parts haven't changed much. Naggar still has that one good momo place that's been the same momo place for fifteen years. The apple orchard road past Pirdi is still empty after 7pm. The temple in Vashisht still has the same priest, who once told me at age twelve that I should "concentrate more in school", advice I have continued to ignore.

I went on a small walk above Old Manali on day two. There's a path past the wooden houses, into the pine forest, that comes out above the river. Half an hour each way. No tourists. The afternoon light coming through the deodars does something to your brain that no podcast on focus will ever replicate.

Came back to the laptop on day five with the kind of clarity that lasts about ninety minutes after you reopen Slack. But for those ninety minutes, the work felt different. Lighter. Less serious in the way that lets you be more serious.

I'll go again in October. It's the other good month, when the apples are off the trees and the valley smells like woodsmoke for three weeks straight. If you've never been in October, plan it.
